Silicon, from the inside out

The shape of
computation.

Why do CPU, GPU, and neural-processor regions look so different on the same chip?

An interactive explanation · Move the sliders to explore.

A chip contains billions of transistors, but its surface doesn’t look like one uniform mass. It has distinct regions, each with its own arrangement of shapes. What’s behind those differences?

Let’s start with a small circuit, make copies of it, and see what happens. Then we’ll build toward memory, CPU cores, and the repeated groups inside a GPU. Each step gives us another clue for reading the bigger picture.1

01 / THE BUILDING BLOCKS

Repetition makes a texture.

Chip designers don’t draw every circuit from scratch. They work with libraries of small, reusable circuits called standard cells. One might flip a signal from 0 to 1. Another chooses between two inputs. Another holds a value. Many fit into rows of the same height, with wider cells taking up more room along the row.12

Switch between mixed logic and a repeated memory cell below, then pull back. The individual pieces gradually disappear, leaving the pattern they make together.

Even the mixed logic sits in tidy rows. Its variety comes from which cells occupy those rows and how they connect. A placement grid can be orderly while the work happening inside it is quite varied.

Repeat the same cell, though, and the whole area begins to look like fabric. That texture is a clue—but it doesn’t tell the whole story.

A very regular patch of silicon might spend its life remembering bits. Let’s see why memory makes such a distinctive pattern.

02 / KEEPING A BIT

Memory wants to be a grid.

Start with a circuit that remembers one bit: a 0 or a 1. A common SRAM cell does this with six transistors. Put lots of these cells together and there’s room to remember something useful.3

Of course, remembering a bit only helps if we can find it again. Rows of cells share selection wires, and columns share wires that carry data. A decoder picks a row, while circuits around the edges handle selecting, reading, and writing the data. A grid makes all of that easier to organize.4

Move through the addresses below and watch the highlighted row and column meet. Change a bit, visit another address, then come back. It’s still there.

This arrangement gives memory its characteristic shape: a dense rectangle of cells with supporting circuitry around the edges. Larger memories often repeat that arrangement in separate banks, so there are patterns within patterns.34

Memory also turns up throughout a processor. CPU cores need caches and registers. GPU groups need places to keep their working data. Queues and buffers hold things until the next circuit is ready for them. Those gold patches in our imaginary chip are scattered around because nearly every part needs somewhere to put things.568

That’s why a stripe inside a GPU block might be a register file or cache. The label describes the whole group; the smaller shapes show some of the circuits inside it.

03 / ORGANIZING DIFFERENT WORK

A CPU needs more than arithmetic.

An adder has a fairly straightforward job: give it two numbers and it adds them. Building a CPU means dealing with everything around that addition. Which instruction comes next? Are its inputs ready? Is one of them still on its way from memory? Where should the answer go?

A fast CPU has circuits for all of this. It fetches and decodes instructions, tracks which operations depend on others, schedules work, and accesses memory. It can even execute instructions out of order, while keeping track of enough information to commit their results in the order the program requires. The open-source BOOM core is a real example of this machinery.5

Move the slider to follow those jobs through the diagram. Each stop represents a responsibility; the slider isn’t counting clock cycles.

All that coordination takes space. Instruction handling, storage, scheduling, and arithmetic need different circuits, so a CPU core develops a fairly varied interior.5

Zoom out, though, and the repetition returns. There may be several similar arithmetic units inside a core—and several copies of the entire core on the chip.

The pattern depends on how far back we stand. A closer look reveals the different circuits inside each repeated block.

04 / REPEATING THE WORKERS

What happens when the work repeats?

Suppose the same calculation needs to run on 64 independent values. That’s a good opportunity to share some of the instruction-handling work. Several execution lanes can follow a common instruction, each working on its own data.

GPUs organize much of their work this way. NVIDIA, for example, groups execution resources with registers, shared memory, and caches into units called streaming multiprocessors. Then it puts many of those groups on a chip.67

Add groups below and run the jobs. Each group in this little model has four lanes, along with its own instruction handling and storage.

Watch what gets copied as the group count rises. Every new group brings a whole bundle of circuits. Repeat that bundle across a chip and the rows and tiles in a GPU region start to make sense.6

Sharing instructions works especially well when the lanes can follow the same path through a program. When their work branches in different directions, things get less tidy. GPUs still need scheduling and control circuitry to handle that.7

Our model gives every job the same easy ride: no waiting for memory, no branching, no uneven workloads. Eight lanes finish 64 jobs in eight rounds; 32 lanes finish in two. Notice how adding parallel workers also repeats their supporting structures.

05 / ARRANGING THE DATA FLOW

Let the numbers travel.

Matrix multiplication involves a lot of repeated arithmetic. Each answer comes from multiplying pairs of numbers and adding the products together. The operation is simple enough; keeping all those arithmetic circuits supplied with numbers is a bigger challenge.

Cij ← Cij + Aik × BkjOne multiply–accumulate, often shortened to MAC.

One approach is to pass values between neighboring cells. Each cell does a little work, then sends the values onward so the next cell can use them too. This is the idea behind a systolic array, used in hardware such as Google’s TPU.89

Press play below, or advance one step at a time. Values from A enter on the left and move right. Values from B enter at the top and move down. Each square keeps a running sum for one answer.

The grid now has a reason to exist. Its cells repeat the same operation, and their neighbors are exactly where the data needs to go next. Short connections let values move through the calculation with relatively little travel.9

A complete accelerator still needs buffers, output storage, control circuits, and hardware for other kinds of work. Google’s TPU, for example, includes vector and scalar units alongside its matrix units.8

Different neural processors make different choices, and GPUs can contain matrix hardware too. This array shows one way repeated calculations can produce a regular physical layout. It doesn’t tell us which design is inside the chip in the example image.7

06 / THE COST OF DISTANCE

Moving a rectangle changes the circuit.

At this point, arranging the blocks might look like a packing puzzle. There’s another constraint: the wires have to work.

Drag the memory away from the CPU below. They still have the same jobs, but now the signals have farther to travel.

In this simple model, making the wire longer increases both its resistance and its capacitance. Their product grows with the square of the length, so doubling the wire makes the unbuffered wire-delay term four times as large. The extra capacitance also takes more energy to charge.10

Real chips have ways to manage this. Designers can add repeaters, use wider wires or different metal layers, or divide a long connection into stages. Each choice comes with tradeoffs in space, timing, and power.10

Placement tools have to juggle those tradeoffs while leaving enough room to route the connections. Two blocks might fit beautifully side by side on a drawing and still make life difficult for everything that needs to connect to them.1112

Moving a rectangle can change how well the chip works. The floorplan is part of the electrical design.

07 / WHAT THE SURFACE HIDES

A chip is not a flat drawing.

There’s more going on above those blocks, too. The devices sit beneath layers of metal carrying local connections, longer routes, and power.13

Separate the stack below. The functional blocks stay at the bottom while the wiring lifts away. Some of the patterns visible from above belong to those connections.

This helps explain why different pictures of a chip can tell different stories. An architecture diagram explains what the parts do. A floorplan shows where they sit. A die image shows a physical surface, which may hide much of what’s underneath.61113

Before assigning a job to a mysterious rectangle, it helps to establish what kind of picture it appears in—and which level of the design it shows.

08 / BACK TO THE BEGINNING

Now look at the example image.

The example image should look a little more familiar now. Large compartments, repeated strips, arrays of small shapes—we’ve seen how each of those patterns can emerge from circuits doing useful work.

Example image, cropped to the chip illustration.

There’s still plenty the picture can’t tell us. A small rectangle could represent a memory bank, a group of arithmetic units, or an entire compute tile. A presentation graphic may also simplify or rearrange details to make its point.

The patterns give us possibilities to investigate. Documentation and physical evidence help establish which explanation actually fits.

Different work creates different structures.
Repeating those structures creates a texture.
Connecting them creates a floorplan.

That’s how parts built from many of the same basic ingredients can end up looking so different on the same chip.
